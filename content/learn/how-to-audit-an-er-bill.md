The size of an ER bill tells you nothing about its accuracy. To audit one: get the itemized bill, match every line against your insurer's explanation of benefits, pull your medical records, then challenge any visit level, trauma fee, or charge the documentation doesn't support ***IN WRITING***. Federal law already protects you from most out-of-network ER charges.

ER bills run high for a specific, checkable set of reasons. This guide walks through each one, then gives you the audit sequence and the exact scripts.

## Why did one ER visit turn into two bills?

An emergency visit usually produces two separate charges: a **facility fee** - the hospital's price for the room, the nurses, and keeping the doors open around the clock - and a **professional fee** for the clinicians who treated you. They can arrive as two separate bills from two separate companies, because the ER clinicians may work for a physician group that bills on its own. Facility fees are billed on top of what you pay the clinician, and the hospital industry defends them as what keeps 24/7 services like the ER running.

Splitting the bill can result in huge errors. In a [KFF Health News "Bill of the Month" case](https://kffhealthnews.org/news/article/the-doctor-didnt-show-up-but-the-hospital-er-still-charged-1012/), a Missouri family took their toddler to a children's hospital ER after he burned himself on an electric stove. A nurse practitioner checked him; the surgeon they were told to wait for never showed, so after more than an hour they went home. The bill ended up being $1,012 with a $192 professional fee plus an $820 facility fee. Appeals went nowhere until reporters started asking questions; then the hospital dropped the $820. Results like that vary; the lesson doesn't: the facility fee is usually the biggest number on the page, and it's auditable.

So your first move is to gather the full set: hospital bill, physician-group bill, and your insurer's explanation of benefits (EOB - the statement showing what your plan paid and what you owe) for each.

One fear you can retire now. The [No Surprises Act](https://www.cms.gov/medical-bill-rights), in effect since January 1, 2022, protects you from unexpected out-of-network bills for emergency room visits, for related care at in-network facilities, and for air ambulances. (Ground ambulances are not on that list.) If an out-of-network ER doctor is balance-billing you, start with our [No Surprises Act guide](/learn/no-surprises-act-explained).

## What's an ER "visit level" - and does yours fit?

Emergency departments score every visit on a five-level intensity scale, and that score largely sets the price. On your itemized bill it shows up as a code number with the ER evaluation codes running from 99281 through 99285, with level 5 (99285) being the most intense. The codes have official definitions we won't reproduce here; you can look any code number up yourself in [CMS's public Physician Fee Schedule tool](https://www.cms.gov/medicare/physician-fee-schedule/search).

Here's why this deserves your attention. A [Peterson-KFF Health System Tracker analysis](https://www.healthsystemtracker.org/brief/outpatient-visits-are-increasingly-billed-at-higher-levels-implications-for-health-costs/) of employer-plan claims found that level 5 emergency department claims grew from 8% of ED claims in 2004 to 25% in 2021 - and that by 2021, level 4 had become the single most common charge at 35%. Some of that is real: sicker patients, urgent care absorbing the minor stuff. Some of it is billing intensity.

To be clear about what you're auditing: it is not the medicine. Whether you needed the CT scan is a clinical call, and it isn't yours to re-litigate. The audit question is narrower and completely fair: **does the visit documentation support the level billed?** A 20-minute visit that ended with a prescription and a discharge sheet can be exactly the right care - and still not support a top-level charge. Asking the hospital to show its work is not an accusation. It's arithmetic.

## What is a critical-care charge?

Separate from the five levels, there's a critical-care category reserved for treating immediately life-threatening conditions, and billed based on time the clinician spends. The code number 99291 is the one you'll usually see for this type of care. Viral posts claim hospitals routinely tack it onto sprained ankles. We can't verify that this is routine, so treat it as an allegation. What we can do is warn you about it. Make sure to look for critical care codes and determine whether it was assigned in error. This is exactly the kind of charge the record either supports or doesn't: it requires a documented life-threatening condition and documented time.

If it appears on your bill and your visit didn't feel life-threatening, ask:

> "My bill includes a critical-care charge. Please show me where my record documents the critical illness or injury treated and the critical-care time spent. If it isn't documented, please review and correct the charge."

## What is a trauma activation fee?

A trauma activation fee is a standby charge for mobilizing a trauma team - often triggered before you arrive, based on the ambulance radio call. When the system works, that team saves lives. The fee still has to match what actually happened.

A [2018 Vox/Kaiser Health News investigation](https://kffhealthnews.org/news/how-er-bills-can-balloon-by-as-much-as-50k-for-trauma-response/) found that when it comes to trauma activation fees, it is "like the Wild West," in the words of UCSF emergency physician Renee Hsia. These fees can range from $1,112 to $50,659 depending on the hospital. The signature case: a family visiting from South Korea took their 8-month-old to Zuckerberg San Francisco General after he fell off a hotel bed. He was fine. He drank some formula, took a nap, and went home. The bill: $18,836 - $15,666 of it a trauma response fee. The hospital waived it, but only after the story went national. Your mileage will vary; your right to ask questions won't.

If a trauma fee is on your bill, ask what activated the team, whether a trauma team actually evaluated you, and where that's documented.

## Here's a summary table of what we've been over so far.

| Charge on the bill | What it is | The question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Facility fee | The hospital's charge for using the ER itself | Does the documented visit support this level? |
| Professional fee | The clinicians' separate charge | Same question - it's leveled too |
| Visit level (1-5) | The intensity score driving both fees | What in my record supports this level? |
| Critical care | Time-based charge for life-threatening treatment | Where are the condition and time documented? |
| Trauma activation | Standby fee for mobilizing a trauma team | What triggered it, and is it documented? |

## How do I actually audit the bill? The 5-step sequence

1. **Get the itemized bill - for both the facility fee and the professional fee (if 2 bills).** The summary bill hides everything that matters. Request an itemized bill with billing codes from the hospital *and* from the physician group. Full walkthrough: [how to get an itemized hospital bill](/learn/how-to-get-an-itemized-hospital-bill). The ask is one sentence:

   > "Please send me an itemized bill for account number __ , listing every charge with its billing code and date of service."

2. **Match every line to your EOB.** Your EOB shows the negotiated price and your actual share - the bill's sticker number is not the final word. If the bill demands more than the EOB's "patient responsibility," stop and investigate before paying anything. Here's [how to read an EOB](/learn/how-to-read-an-eob) and [how to tell if you were overcharged](/learn/how-to-know-if-you-were-overcharged).

3. **Pull your medical records.** Federal law gives you the right to copies of your records, generally within 30 days of your request ([45 CFR 164.524](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-164/subpart-E/section-164.524)). For an ER visit, request the visit note, triage and nursing notes, orders, the medication administration record, and the discharge summary. Then trace every billed line to something documented. We wrote a full guide: [how to verify your bill with medical records](/learn/how-to-verify-your-bill-with-medical-records).

4. **Put the level question in writing.** Phone calls evaporate; letters get answered. Send the billing department something like:

   > "My bill charges a level __ visit [and a critical-care / trauma activation fee]. Having reviewed my medical records, I don't see documentation supporting that level of service. Please provide the supporting documentation, or review and rebill at the documented level. Please send a corrected itemized bill and pause collection activity while this is reviewed."

   Keep copies of everything. If they answer "that's our protocol," hold the line: a protocol is ***NOT*** documentation of *your* visit.

5. **Pick your endgame.** If the insurer processed the claim wrong or denied it, that's an [appeal](/learn/how-to-appeal-a-denied-health-insurance-claim). If the charges survive the audit but the price is still crushing, [negotiate](/learn/how-to-negotiate-a-medical-bill) and check [charity care and financial assistance](/learn/hospital-charity-care-financial-assistance). If you're being stonewalled, escalate - here's [who to complain to about a medical bill](/learn/who-to-complain-to-about-a-medical-bill).

## FAQ

### Can the ER bill me even though I left without being seen?

Leaving early doesn't erase charges for whatever happened before you walked out - the Missouri family above saw only a nurse practitioner, gave up on a no-show surgeon, and still got a $1,012 bill. But those charges are auditable: ask what assessment is actually documented and whether it supports the level billed. Their facility fee was ultimately dropped, though results vary.

### Do I have to pay an out-of-network ER doctor's balance bill?

Generally no. Since January 1, 2022, the No Surprises Act protects patients with health insurance from unexpected out-of-network bills for ER visits, related care at in-network facilities, and air ambulances - though ground ambulances aren't on the list, per [CMS](https://www.cms.gov/medical-bill-rights). If you're being balance-billed for emergency care, see our [No Surprises Act guide](/learn/no-surprises-act-explained).

### Is questioning the visit level accusing my doctor of lying?

No. You aren't questioning the care - that's a clinical call, and it stays with your clinicians. You're asking whether the documentation supports the charge. That's a billing question, and putting it in writing is a routine, legitimate ask.

### What if the bill is accurate but I can't afford it?

An audit that comes back clean isn't the end. Many hospitals have financial assistance policies that can reduce or erase the balance - here's [how charity care works](/learn/hospital-charity-care-financial-assistance) - and a lump-sum or payment-plan [negotiation](/learn/how-to-negotiate-a-medical-bill) is always on the table.
